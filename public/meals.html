Eleven chicken styles

Every batch is 5 lb of boneless skinless breast cut into 10 portions (about 175 g cooked chicken plus a tenth of the sauce). Per-portion macros are the whole batch's ingredients divided by 10. The red number is how much more each portion costs than your plain 6 oz of cooked chicken (281 kcal); the switcher takes that out of the carb.

Four turkey options

99% lean turkey is breast meat at about 1% fat and dries out easily. It does best in saucy dishes, with grated onion or mushroom mixed in, at no more than medium-high heat, broken into larger crumbles, and taken to 165°F and no further (Food Republic; Tasting Table).

Cut every 5 lb batch into 16 portions (about 4 oz cooked each). Mix the sauce through, weigh the whole batch, divide by 16 and fill each container to that weight. Meatballs: make 96 (6 per portion), or weigh the cooked tray and divide by 16.

Carb and veg swaps

Swap carbs at equal calories by weight, not cups. The switcher does the math, but the rule is simple: grams to keep = calories left for the carb ÷ kcal per gram. Every carb value comes from the USDA FoodData Central record in the source column, read through a mirror site.

Black beans and quinoa trade some carbs for 3 to 10 g more protein and much more fiber. Potatoes give the biggest plate per calorie; pasta and naan give the smallest. Carb Balance tortillas are effectively a low-carb day.

Veg

The default veg portion is the weight that matches 1 cup of cooked broccoli (55 kcal), so most swaps barely move the carb. The switcher counts the exact difference anyway.

Roasting broccoli in oil nearly doubles it to 67 kcal/100 g (GetFoodFacts). Measure the oil and take it out of the carb.

Pairings (cuisine convention, not a source): tinga, salsa verde, cilantro-lime and taco turkey with corn tortillas, rice or black beans plus peppers or zucchini. Buffalo with russet or baby potatoes and carrots, or a Carb Balance wrap. BBQ and hot honey with sweet potato and green beans. Honey-garlic, gochujang and Thai peanut with jasmine rice or rice noodles and broccoli or green beans. Greek lemon and Italian turkey with pita, couscous, orzo or farro plus zucchini or asparagus. Butter chicken with basmati or naan and cauliflower.

Bulk carb cooking

White or jasmine rice: rinse, 1:1 water, 3 min High, 10 min NR; doubling or tripling uses the same time (Pressure Cook Recipes). Brown rice: 1:1, 15 min High, 5 min NR (PCR). Quinoa: 1:1, 0 to 1 min, 10 to 12 min NR (PCR). Baby potatoes 5 min, medium Yukons 6 min, with 1 cup water in a 6 qt or 2 cups in an 8 qt (Spice Cravings). Whole sweet potatoes on a trivet with 1½ cups water: 20 min for 2" thick, 30 min for 3 to 4", then 10 NR (Wholefully).

Rice and potatoes can't share a pot with the chicken, so run them after the protein cycle or roast potatoes while the pot works. Weigh each cooked carb batch and portion by grams; cup measures drift with water absorption.

Portioning and Instant Pot rules

Split each batch into a set number of equal containers and count macros from the ingredients. Cooking loss, sauce reduction and how much juice the chicken releases then don't affect your numbers.

  • Chicken: 2 portions per pound raw, so 10 per 5 lb. A 4.6 lb pack makes 9, and the per-portion macros stay about the same.
  • 99% turkey: about 3 portions per pound raw, so 16 per 5 lb.
  1. Shred the meat into its sauce and stir well so the sauce is evenly mixed.
  2. Weigh the whole pot, subtract the empty pot weight, and divide by 10 (or 16 for turkey). That's your portion weight.
  3. Weigh each container to that number. The last one will be a little over or under, and that's fine.

If you'd rather keep weighing 6 oz of meat, lift the meat out of the sauce, weigh 170 g into each container, then divide the sauce evenly. Don't weigh 6 oz of sauced meat. Cooked meat values vary about ±5% with how dry you cook it, so being consistent about what you weigh matters more than the exact number.

Pressure time follows thickness

Frozen-breast timings run 11 to 12 min for 6 to 8 oz pieces, 13 to 14 min for 9 to 10 oz and 16 min for 12 oz, each with a 10 min natural release, and the number of breasts doesn't change the time (Kristine's Kitchen). For 5 lb of fresh breasts, use 10 to 12 min High plus 10 min natural release (NR), and expect 15 to 25 min to come up to pressure. Halve any breast over about 12 oz. Frozen breasts must be separate pieces, not a block. Stacking 2 to 3 layers works, but check that the top layer reads 165°F.

Avoiding BURN

Thin liquid on the bottom, then the meat, then thick sauce on top, not stirred. Minimum thin liquid is 1½ cups in a 6 qt and 2 cups in an 8 qt; tomato products and thick sauces don't count. Deglaze after any sauté, and never add cornstarch or flour before pressure cooking (Paint the Kitchen Red). Breasts release a lot of juice, so a 5 lb batch still needs only the pot minimum. Thickeners and dairy (slurry, yogurt, butter, peanut powder) go in after the pressure cycle.

The 5 lb scaling is arithmetic from the cited 1.5 to 2.5 lb recipes; no source tested these styles at 5 lb. Treat the first batch of each as calibration: time it, check 165°F in the thickest piece, and split it into the set number of portions.

Sous vide and air fryer

For 5 lb of breasts, use 4 to 5 gallon bags laid flat in a single layer (Breville), 150°F for 2½ to 3 h, ice bath, then straight into the freezer in the same sealed bags. Bag 2 breasts (about 1 lb raw, 2 portions) per bag. Reheat in a 150°F bath for 30 to 45 min from thawed, or about 1 h from frozen. The air fryer is a finisher: about 1 lb of cutlets per round at 375 to 380°F for 10 to 14 min (Dash for Dinner). It's best for re-crisping a reheated portion of buffalo, BBQ or tinga for 3 to 5 min (unsourced, so watch the first batch).

Freeze everything the day you cook

Cooked poultry keeps 2 to 6 months frozen for best quality, and frozen food stays safe indefinitely (FoodSafety.gov). Rotate each batch within 2 to 3 months for best taste. Freezing doesn't change the macros.

  1. Cool fast. Into the fridge within 2 hours (1 hour above 90°F). Split a full pot into shallow containers, or set the insert in ice water and stir 10 to 15 min first (USDA FSIS).
  2. Portion, then freeze. Fill to the portion weight, leave ½ inch of headspace, and chill an hour or two before the freezer. Ten warm containers at once warms the freezer.
  3. Label each container with style, date, kcal and protein from the cards above.
  4. Thaw overnight in the fridge and eat within 3 to 4 days, or microwave from frozen. Reheat to 165°F, covered, stirring halfway.
  5. Keep some liquid with it. A few tablespoons of sauce or cooking liquid per container prevents freezer burn and dry chicken.

Rice. Bacillus cereus spores survive cooking, and slow cooling lets them make a heat-stable toxin that reheating won't destroy. Cool rice from 135°F to 70°F within 2 hours and to 41°F within 4 more by spreading it thin on a sheet pan, then portion and freeze. Reheat only the portion you're about to eat (NC State Extension).

Carbs. Rice, quinoa, farro and couscous freeze very well, so pack them with the chicken and add 1 tbsp water before microwaving. Pasta and orzo freeze fine if cooked 1 to 2 min short. Sweet potatoes freeze well mashed or cubed. Plain boiled or baked white potatoes go watery and grainy, so cook those fresh or buy frozen roasted potatoes. Freeze tortillas, pita and naan separately from saucy meat.

Veg. Cooked broccoli goes mushy. Pack containers with frozen raw broccoli (it steams as the meal reheats; pick "Broccoli, frozen" in the switcher), or steam it 2 min before freezing. Green beans, carrots and cauliflower hold up best; zucchini and asparagus go soft.

Sous vide shelf life. USDA data shows lean chicken is safe after 2.7 min at 150°F or 8.9 min at 145°F (America's Test Kitchen, citing FSIS). Baldwin's tables pasteurize 50 mm breast in about 2¼ h at 149°F, and sealed pouches chilled in an ice bath and held below 38°F keep 3 to 4 weeks by his guidance, or up to a year frozen (Baldwin). Anova says "at least a week" (Anova). USDA doesn't endorse the longer times and many home fridges run warmer than 38°F. Once a bag is opened, the 3 to 4 day rule applies.

Where the numbers come from

Your fixed foods and the plain meats use the values from your original doc (USDA FoodData Central SR Legacy records read through a mirror site). The peanut butter and olive oil added in the cut plan use standard USDA values for natural smooth peanut butter (about 597 kcal/100 g) and olive oil (119 kcal/tbsp), not re-fetched. Sauce macros are calculations from ingredient lists using standard label values (appendix below); treat them as ±10% and check them against the labels you buy.
